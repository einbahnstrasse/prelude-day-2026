Twist the Dial: Same Notes, New Worlds

Prelude Day — UWM Peck School of the Arts — October 23, 2026

Dies irae (“Day of Wrath”)

source: Verbum Gloriae, “Dies iræ – Secuencia de la Misa de Difuntos”

  • Chant from the Requiem Mass, sung in Latin for the dead.
  • 13th century (earliest known source is a Franciscan missal, c. 1253–55).
  • Notice that its melody keeps returning home to one note: D.
  • Medieval musicians called that home note the final — and sorted chants into modes that share the same final.
  • This one we now call Dorian mode.

Dies irae: Modern Notation

source: Wikimedia Commons, “Dies Irae Treble.JPG” (public domain)

Same melody, modern treble clef: now we can see it start, circle, and land on D.

White Keys — D Final

White keys only, but D is home (“final”). That collection is called D Dorian.

Wrap 12 chromatic pitches around a circle: the same 7 white keys, with D on top.

Clockwise from D: W H W W W H W (whole & half steps). That pattern is Dorian.

What Is a Mode?

A mode is a collection of notes plus a final: one note that all others revolve around.

Same white keys, but now C is the final: our familiar major scale, also called C Ionian.

Twist the dial so that C is on top: the same 7 white keys, but a new center.

Clockwise from C: W W H W W W H. Compare D Dorian: W H W W W H W. Same notes, new final, new world.

Building Triads

Stack every other note of the scale (a third apart) to build a triad on each step.

3 flavors appear: major (I, IV, V), minor (ii, iii, vi), and diminished (vii°). Each mode puts them in a different order around its final.

Triads in D Dorian

Same white keys, same stacking — but now we count from D, the final.

Move the final and you move the center of gravity: every note and chord now leans toward D, not C. Same pieces, a whole new world.

Dorian’s fingerprint: a minor i, but a major IV (G–B–D), thanks to B♮, the raised 6th.

The Swiss Army Knife

Few materials, endless tools: just move the final, and the same pitches slice, dice, and do it all!

Modes are thus the family of scales built on the same collection of pitches, each with a different final. They are modes of one another, not isolated scales.

  • C Ionian (major)
  • D Dorian
  • E Phrygian
  • F Lydian
  • G Mixolydian
  • A Aeolian (natural minor)
  • B Locrian

Twist the dial: same notes, new sense of key, new center of gravity, a whole new world.

So What (Miles Davis, 1959)

source: dancohen, “Animated Sheet Music: ‘So What’ by Miles Davis”

  • For centuries, these few materials have given composers a toolkit of resources.
  • 700 years after Dies irae, the Dorian mode inspired jazz greats Miles Davis (trp.) and John Coltrane (sax.).
  • 16 bars of D Dorian, 8 bars a half step up in E♭ Dorian, then 8 more in D.
  • Listen for a stable final on D, and wide-open space to improvise.

Triads in E Phrygian

E Phrygian: same white keys, home on E. Its signature is the half step above home: F♮ pulling down to E. Same stacking, but now we count from E, the mode’s final.

Phrygian’s fingerprint: a major II (F–A–C) just a half step above the minor i, thanks to F♮, the lowered 2nd.

And the v is diminished (B°): no strong dominant to pull us home, so the ♭2 does the pulling instead. If we want to create a strong pull back to E, we can also raise the leading tone from D to D#, just as we do in other minor scales.

Love Ridden (Fiona Apple, 1999)

source: Fiona Apple, “Love Ridden” (official audio)

  • Listen to the piano rocking between E minor and F major: this is the Phrygian ♭2 leaning on home.
  • Where does the music feel most at home? Where does it pull away?

Love Ridden (Fiona Apple)

Before a word is sung, the piano tells us where home is: E minor, nudged by F major.

Bars 1–4: E minor, then F — built on ♭2 — sinking back toward E. Pure Phrygian color!

Bars 5–8: the F becomes B7/D♯, and a D♯ leading tone pulls up to E. Does that altered note change our musical world, or just pull us toward home?

Love Ridden: The Voice Enters

The voice floats on G, A, and B (reaching up to D once). It never even sings E: the piano holds home underneath.

Listen to the bass: under “rid-den,” F♮ (the ♭2) leans down on E; under “gave,” D♯ pushes up into it.

Love Ridden: First Phrase

The harmony widens: G to F to C to Cmaj7 to a half cadence on B7.
On “brow,” the voice finally sinks to E: home at last.

Then B7, with its D♯ leading tone, leans hard back toward E:
the tonal pull from inside a Phrygian world...

Love Ridden: Chorus

The chords step down: G to F to Em. The F♮ chord (Phrygian ♭2) slides into home.

Love Ridden: Off the Dial

Now the bass slides down in half steps, G, F♯, E, E♭, D, stepping off our 7 white keys.

Composers may leave the collection sometimes but then come back to create variety.

Modes of Limited Transposition

Olivier Messiaen: French composer, organist, catalogued a special family of scales.

So far, we've looked some of the church modes: one collection (white keys), many finals. Twist the dial, and a new note becomes home base.

Messiaen’s modes: scales are built from a repeating pattern. Transpose a scale, and very soon you land back on the very same notes.

A major scale has 12 different transpositions; you can play the same scale in 12 different keys. But Messiaen's scales have only a few: hence limited transposition.

With no single note in charge, home becomes ambiguous: a new kind of world...

Mode 3 on the Dial

Messiaen Mode 3: a simple 3-note pattern,
W–H–H (2–1–1), transposed three times:
C D E♭, E F♯ G, A♭ B♭ B — 9 notes total.

  • Up a half step: 6 notes stay, 3 swap
  • Up again: 6 stay, 3 swap
  • And again: 6 stay, 3 swap
  • Once more and… we’re back where we started!

Same 9 notes as T0, rotated to start on E.

A Musical Palindrome

Count Mode 3 in half steps starting from E♭:

1 2 1 1 2 1 1 2 1

Same forwards and backwards, just like:

R A C E C A R

On the dial, it’s a 3-bladed pinwheel:
turn it a third of the way around (C to E to A♭) and it looks exactly the same!

Messiaen called this “the charm of impossibilities.”

Chords in Mode 3

Build a triad on each step, using only the mode’s 9 notes.

Messiaen, Ex. 333: each voice climbs the entire mode, starting on a different degree, then retraces its steps.

6 major and 3 augmented triads: familiar chords in an unfamiliar, symmetrical world.

In Ex. 333, the mode’s symmetry shapes the harmony:
chords 1, 4, 7, and 10 are the same sound, each a major third higher.

Theme et variations
(Messiaen, 1932)

source: George N. Gianopoulos, “Olivier Messiaen – Theme and Variations for Violin and Piano (1932) [Score-Video]”

  • A wedding gift for his wife, violinist Claire Delbos; they premiered it together in 1932.
